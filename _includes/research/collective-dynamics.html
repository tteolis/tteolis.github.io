Transformer inference dynamics

What happens to a prompt’s token representations as they pass through a transformer? In work with Sixu Li, Thomas Jacob Maranzatto, Jan Peszek, Semih Akkoc, Konstantin Riedl, Sennur Ulukus, and Nicolás García Trillos, we study these inference dynamics through interacting particle systems and collective dynamics. Attention mixes information between tokens; repeated mixing can produce clusters, collapse, or persistent motion. Understanding these patterns helps us ask what information a deep network preserves.

From my PhD: collective dynamics and fluid limits

How does coordinated motion emerge from local interactions? Collective dynamics involves flocking, swarming, synchronization, and self-organization. During my PhD, I studied how individual interactions become collective motion and how large particle systems turn into fluid equations, involving kinetic theory, multiscale modeling, and the emergence of macroscopic behavior.

Particles suspended in a fluid

The Fokker–Planck–Navier–Stokes (FPNS) system couples moving particles to a viscous, incompressible fluid. Particles align through Cucker–Smale interactions, feel drag and thermal noise, and push back on the fluid. In work with my advisor, Roman Shvydkoy, we use hypocoercivity to prove exponential synchronization for the locally smoothed drag model: the fluid approaches a constant velocity and the particle distribution approaches a Maxwellian centered at the same velocity. This holds without a small-initial-data assumption or an additional assumption that the evolving solution stays uniformly controlled for all time; the needed bounds follow from the model and the initial-data conditions.

Simulation setup

The kinetic Fokker–Planck part is approximated by 96 stochastic particles: their positions follow their velocities, and their velocities evolve under alignment, fluid drag, and Brownian kicks representing velocity diffusion. These particles exchange momentum with a 24 × 24 incompressible Fourier fluid grid on the periodic unit square. Communication and two-way drag are spatially smoothed. A nonzero bulk drift and low positive temperature σ = 0.0004 make the shared direction visible. The integration step is 0.002, checked against 0.004 with coupled Brownian increments. The fluid field is projected to remain divergence-free; particle–fluid momentum exchange is balanced.

Hydrodynamic limits: from many velocities to a fluid

A hydrodynamic limit replaces a detailed distribution of particle positions and velocities by a few fields, such as density and fluid velocity. The idea comes from deriving fluid equations from molecular motion: local interactions act much faster than the large-scale flow, so they quickly establish a local equilibrium.

For an aligning population, rapid local relaxation pulls nearby velocities toward their local mean. If thermal noise also vanishes, the natural equilibrium has one velocity at each position: a monokinetic distribution. This is the cold, pressureless counterpart of the Maxwellian equilibrium used in gas dynamics. Writing ρ for density, u for velocity, and δu for a point mass at u, the limit f → ρδu leads to pressureless Euler alignment.

With Alina Chertock and Roman Shvydkoy, we ask a finer question: what shape survives inside the narrowing velocity distribution? The profile g(t,x,ξ) is that distribution viewed under a magnifying glass: ξ measures velocity relative to a moving local center, divided by its shrinking width. The density is rescaled to preserve mass. With vanishing noise and strong relaxation, its velocity shape becomes Gaussian; without noise, it obeys a transport equation and remembers its initial shape. These results use regularity and non-oscillation assumptions.

The s-model: adaptive communication strength

In the s-model, interaction strength is an evolving field s, allowing a flock to adapt its communication. With Roman Shvydkoy, we built the particle and kinetic descriptions and established their mean-field and hydrodynamic limits. The result connects individual agents to Euler-type fluid equations, in both the monokinetic and Maxwellian regimes.

How does communication strength shape collective motion in populations of different sizes and masses? In the s-model, adaptive communication strength allows it to align locally while remaining far from the heavy flock.

The pressureless fluid limit
∂tρ+∇⋅(ρu)=0,∂ts+∇⋅(suˉ)=0,∂tu+(u⋅∇)u=s(uˉ−u).\begin{aligned}\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)&=0,\\ \partial_t s+\nabla\cdot(s\bar u)&=0,\\ \partial_t u+(u\cdot\nabla)u&=s(\bar u-u).\end{aligned}

Here ρ and u are the flock density and velocity, s is communication strength, and ū is the locally averaged velocity. For the Cucker–Smale averaging rule, ū = [φ * (ρu)]/(φ * ρ), where * denotes convolution. The mean-field limit sends particle number to infinity; the hydrodynamic limit imposes rapid local velocity equilibration. The results use smooth averaging kernels and a smooth limiting fluid solution on the time interval considered.
