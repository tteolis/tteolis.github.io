Sampling & particle methods

In machine learning, models are trained by finding parameters that make a loss small. But several different explanations may fit the same data, and a single fitted model can hide that uncertainty. Sampling lets us explore these possibilities and average their predictions. This is central to Bayesian inference, uncertainty quantification, and generative modeling. The same idea appears in statistical physics, where samples describe the molecular configurations a system is likely to visit.

In my paper with Maarten de Hoop, we introduce a sampling method with a finite measure of error that decreases along particle motion (called Green–Bessel). This particle Lyapunov energy lets us prove last-iterate convergence: as the number of particles and observation time grow, the particle cloud approaches the target. Last-iterate convergence is well established for Langevin dynamics and is known for some special deterministic systems. Our contribution is to obtain it for a deterministic interacting-particle method with general smooth strictly positive targets on a compact state space, including multimodal targets, without time averaging, added noise, or log-concavity. The particles may start far from equilibrium, and the approximation is controlled uniformly over all time.

Background: Langevin, SVGD, LAWGD, and Green–Bessel

From gradient descent to sampling

Think of x as a possible set of model parameters and U(x) as a loss landscape. Gradient descent moves downhill. Sampling explores the whole landscape according to a target density π(x) = Z−1e−U(x), where Z makes the density integrate to one. Low-loss regions receive more weight, while their width and volume also matter. For Bayesian inference, U is the negative log-likelihood plus the negative log-prior; in a Gibbs model, it is physical energy scaled by temperature.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) already has randomness because it estimates gradients from minibatches. Under suitable assumptions, its motion can be approximated by a diffusion. Langevin dynamics gives a particularly clean version of this idea: gradient descent with calibrated Brownian noise.

dXi=−∇U(Xi) dt+2 dBi.dX_i=-\nabla U(X_i)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_i.

Here Xi is one evolving candidate, t is time, ∇ is the gradient, and the Bi are independent Brownian motions. The noise helps candidates explore beyond the nearest minimum. Sampling can use one trajectory observed over time, or many trajectories run together. In the particle methods below, a whole cloud represents the distribution and its members interact to improve coverage.

How close is this to ordinary SGD?

Ordinary SGD’s minibatch noise generally depends on the current parameters and has different strengths in different directions, so it does not automatically sample π. Diffusion models of SGD are useful approximations under assumptions on stepsizes and noise (Mandt, Hoffman & Blei). Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics explicitly adds noise to minibatch updates to target a posterior, with suitable stepsize control (Welling & Teh).

The density ρt of Langevin candidates relaxes toward π. Relative entropy Ht = KL(ρt|π) measures distributional error and decreases along this population flow.

Population flow, Fisher information I, and entropy
∂tρ=∇⋅ ⁣(ρ∇log⁡ρπ).\partial_t\rho=\nabla\cdot\!\left(\rho\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right).
I(ρ∣π)=∫∣∇log⁡ρπ∣2ρ dx.I(\rho\mid\pi)=\int\left|\nabla\log\frac{\rho}{\pi}\right|^2\rho\,dx.
H˙=−I(ρ∣π),Ht≤e−2t/CLSH0.\dot H=-I(\rho\mid\pi),\qquad H_t\le e^{-2t/C_{\rm LS}}H_0.

Ht = KL(ρt|π) is the relative entropy, ∫ρt log(ρt/π) dx. The relative Fisher information I = ∫|∇log(ρt/π)|2ρt dx measures mismatch in density gradients. The exponential bound holds when the target has log-Sobolev constant CLS. See the Langevin gradient-flow viewpoint.

Why separated modes are slow

Write the relative density error as u = ρ/π − 1. The Fokker–Planck equation becomes a linear equation for u, introducing the nonnegative Langevin operator Aπ:

∂tu=−Aπu.\partial_t u=-A_\pi u.
Aπu=−1π∇⋅(π∇u).A_\pi u=-\frac1\pi\nabla\cdot(\pi\nabla u).
Aπφk=λkφk.A_\pi\varphi_k=\lambda_k\varphi_k.
ut=∑k≥1ak(0)e−λktφk.u_t=\sum_{k\ge1}a_k(0)e^{-\lambda_k t}\varphi_k.

The eigenfunctions ϕk are natural error patterns, ak(0) their initial amplitudes, and λk their decay rates. Below, the first eigenfunction is nearly constant within each well, with opposite signs. This missing-well imbalance relaxes slowly because λ1 is small: Langevin particles rarely cross the low-density gap, so exchanging probability between wells takes a long time.

SVGD: move a cloud together

Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD) moves particles deterministically, using kernel interactions to spread them out. The kernel k(x,y) couples states x and y. With N particles,

X˙i=1N∑j=1N[k(Xi,Xj)∇log⁡π(Xj)+∇2k(Xi,Xj)].\begin{aligned}\dot X_i=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N [&k(X_i,X_j)\nabla\log\pi(X_j)\\&+\nabla_2 k(X_i,X_j)].\end{aligned}

These terms combine attraction toward probability mass and particle repulsion; ∇2 differentiates the second kernel argument. This is gradient descent over distributions (Liu & Wang; Liu).

Population flow and kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD)
∂tρ+∇⋅(ρvρ)=0.\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho v_\rho)=0.
vρ(x)=∫[k(x,y)∇log⁡π(y)+∇yk(x,y)] ρ(dy).\begin{aligned}v_\rho(x)=\int [&k(x,y)\nabla\log\pi(y)\\&+\nabla_y k(x,y)]\,\rho(dy).\end{aligned}
H˙=−KSD⁡k(ρ,π)2≤0.\dot H=-\operatorname{KSD}_k(\rho,\pi)^2\le0.

The velocity vρ replaces the particle average by an average over the current distribution ρ. KSDk, the kernel Stein discrepancy, is the size of this velocity in the kernel’s space of vector fields:

KSD⁡k(ρ,π)2=∥vρ∥Hkd2.\operatorname{KSD}_k(\rho,\pi)^2=\|v_\rho\|_{\mathcal H_k^d}^2.

Here d is the state dimension and ℋkd is the vector-valued reproducing-kernel Hilbert space generated by k; its norm measures the size of a field using that kernel. The discrepancy vanishes at the target. Equivalently, it is the largest average Stein residual ∇·g + g·∇log π among vector fields g of kernel norm at most one.

LAWGD: give slow errors a boost

LAWGD weights each error component by 1/λk, giving slow, missing-well errors a boost. This “preconditioned Langevin” flow balances relaxation rates near the target, much as preconditioning accelerates an ill-conditioned optimization problem.

LangevinLAWGD, near the target

Remarkably, its population entropy decays at a rate independent of the Poincaré constant, the target’s slow-relaxation timescale (Chewi and collaborators):

H˙=−χ2(ρ∣π)≤−H,Ht≤e−tH0.\dot H=-\chi^2(\rho\mid\pi)\le-H,\qquad H_t\le e^{-t}H_0.

Here χ2(ρ|π) = ∫(ρ/π − 1)2dπ is the squared density-ratio error.

LAWGD flow

The Green kernel G0 implements the inverse of Aπ using the centered target eigenfunctions. Its particle and population velocities are

G0(x,y)=∑k≥1φk(x)φk(y)λk.G_0(x,y)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\varphi_k(x)\varphi_k(y)}{\lambda_k}.
X˙i=−1N∑j∇xG0(Xi,Xj).\dot X_i=-\frac1N\sum_j\nabla_xG_0(X_i,X_j).
vρ(x)=−∫∇xG0(x,y) ρ(dy).v_\rho(x)=-\int\nabla_xG_0(x,y)\,\rho(dy).

The density follows the same continuity equation as above. The entropy bound assumes a positive spectral gap and regular population solutions.

Green–Bessel: an energy for actual particles

We want a finite, decreasing energy for the actual particle cloud. LAWGD’s Green energy diverges on point masses in state dimension d ≥ 2. We retain its Green factor, 1/λk, and add Bessel smoothing for high-frequency errors:

Er(μ)=12∑k≥1∣⟨φk,μ−π⟩∣2λk(1+λk)r.E_r(\mu)=\frac12\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{|\langle\varphi_k,\mu-\pi\rangle|^2}{\lambda_k(1+\lambda_k)^r}.

Here μ is the current distribution; brackets compare averages of ϕk under μ and π, and r sets the smoothing strength. Sufficient smoothing makes the energy finite on particles, while it still vanishes exactly at the target. Its gradient flow gives

ddtEr(μtN)=−1N∑i∣X˙i∣2≤0.\frac{d}{dt}E_r(\mu_t^N)=-\frac1N\sum_i|\dot X_i|^2\le0.

The empirical distribution μtN puts mass 1/N at each particle. This is our particle Lyapunov functional: once its energy is low, it stays low. It provides the uniform long-time control needed for the last-iterate particle convergence result described above.

Population dissipation and scope
H˙=−∑k≥1∣⟨φk,ρt−π⟩∣2(1+λk)r≤−2λ1Er(ρt).\dot H=-\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{|\langle\varphi_k,\rho_t-\pi\rangle|^2}{(1+\lambda_k)^r}\le-2\lambda_1 E_r(\rho_t).

A positive matrix-valued kernel realizes this gradient flow as SVGD. The complete theorem assumes a smooth strictly positive target on a compact connected manifold without boundary, sufficiently large integer r, and independent initialization from a fixed smooth density. It therefore covers periodic targets with multiple wells. The Euclidean extension needs additional regularity and transport estimates.

Earlier last-iterate results include Langevin diffusions, Langevin-regularized SVGD, and special deterministic Gaussian systems. Langevin guarantees concern convergence of the sampling law; they do not require a discrepancy that decreases along every realized particle cloud. The advance here combines deterministic dynamics, general smooth positive compact-state targets, initialization away from equilibrium, and control of arbitrarily late times. The original LAWGD analysis provides a population guarantee; a comparable general last-iterate particle theorem is not supplied there.

A finite, decreasing particle discrepancy is not unprecedented across all sampling methods: MMD gradient flows provide an earlier example. Their formulation uses target kernel averages, which can be approximated from target samples, and a decreasing discrepancy alone does not guarantee global convergence. Our contribution is the particular target-adapted particle energy and the joint long-time, many-particle convergence theorem it enables.

Simulation setup

N = 64 particles. The target energy is U(x) = ∑j=1,2[(xj2−1)2/(2σ2) + xj2/2], where x = (x1, x2) and σ = 0.3 controls well sharpness. The numerical domain is [−1.9,1.9]2. Green–Bessel: smoothing strength r = 2.05 and length ℓ = 0.1. RBF: adaptive median-bandwidth multiplier 5.33484, selected using an independent sampling discrepancy.

The numerical energy uses weights 1/[λ(1+ℓ2λ)r], where ℓ = 0.10 is an adjustable smoothing length. The formula above uses the paper’s convention ℓ = 1. This reflecting-box example with r = 2.05 illustrates the method; the full theorem assumes a compact manifold without boundary and a larger smoothing exponent. Spectral resolution must also increase for an exact target limit.

LAWGD uses the same target eigenfunctions with inverse-eigenvalue weights; RBF uses a Gaussian kernel. Langevin uses independent Brownian noise, with integration step at most 0.001.
