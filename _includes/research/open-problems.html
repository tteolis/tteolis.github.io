Open problems

Last-iterate convergence with standard Stein kernels

Can we prove that deterministic SVGD with standard, non-target-adapted kernels, such as RBF or Riesz kernels, approximates broad classes of targets at any observation time tending to infinity as particle number grows? The goal is a last-iterate guarantee with uniform long-time control, starting from suitable nonequilibrium initial data, without time averaging or added noise.

What is known, and what remains open?

The question concerns the empirical distribution as particle number N tends to infinity, rather than exact equality to a continuous target with finitely many particles. A general joint particle/long-time theorem for these standard deterministic kernels remains open. Shi & Mackey give finite-particle bounds for best or averaged kernel Stein discrepancy; Gaussian systems with bilinear kernels admit stronger special-case results. The bilinear kernel itself is target-independent, so the open question needs this general-target qualification.

For Riesz kernels, mean-field local convergence concerns a population density, while our particle theorem gives time-averaged convergence. Langevin-regularized SVGD has last-iterate guarantees with added noise. These leave the broad deterministic particle question above.

A flock with a blind spot

Here is a simple Cucker–Smale question that still interests me and remains unsolved when there are infinitely many particles: can a flock stay together when close neighbors do not communicate, uniformly as the number of agents grows?

The model, what is known, and simulations

Agent i has position xi and velocity vi. Each agent adjusts its velocity toward those of its neighbors, with communication strength φ(r) depending on their separation r:

x˙i=vi.\dot x_i=v_i.x˙i​=vi​.
v˙i=1N∑jϕ(∣xi−xj∣)(vj−vi).\dot v_i=\frac1N\sum_j\phi(|x_i-x_j|)(v_j-v_i).v˙i​=N1​j∑​ϕ(∣xi​−xj​∣)(vj​−vi​).

Here N is the number of agents. With positive, decreasing, fat-tailed communication, classical flocking theory gives exponential velocity alignment and a bounded flock diameter (Ha & Liu). A fat tail means that communication stays strong enough at long distances to keep distant agents connected.

Now switch off communication within a radius r0, while keeping the long-range tail. Velocity alignment still occurs (Dietert & Shvydkoy). For each fixed finite N, the flock’s diameter remains bounded. The open question is whether this bound can be chosen independently of N, for initial flocks with uniformly bounded position and velocity support. In other words, does the population stay within a bounded diameter for all time? Numerics and intuition suggest yes, but a proof remains elusive with the current tools.
